## Bolter and Grusin on remediation

Jay David Bolter and Richard Grusin, “Remediation,” *Configurations* 4, no. 3 (1996), 311–358. The reading was the opening discussion of *Strange Days*, especially journal pages 313–314. The linked copy reflows the article. Its second PDF page contains the journal page-end markers for 313 and 314. The DOI metadata confirms the article, issue and page range. [Article text](https://www.dss-edit.com/plu/Bolter-Grusin_Remediation.pdf) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1353/con.1996.0018)

The film imagines a recording device whose promise is to let someone experience an event directly. Its own staging, and the film’s debt to other films, leave that promise open to scrutiny. The authors use the example to connect two impulses. A medium can make its construction recede and can draw attention to the several means of representation used to produce an image. Each impulse can sustain the other.

In the bell exhibit, a rendered view borrows the framing and perspective of a camera photograph. The source layer sets the single frame alongside four rendered source images, camera choices and measurements. A rival reading is that the colour map supplies a new appearance of direct access to truth, even as it exposes a technical process. The interface supplies material for comparing those readings. Establishing which reading a viewer adopts would require evidence from viewers. *Remediation* here means media refashioning other media. Repairing a reconstruction or looking up another source is an engineering operation with its own observable outcome. [Original bell on two camera grids](../releases/v0.5.0/bell-comparison/README.md)

## The London Charter on documenting a claim

*The London Charter for the Computer-based Visualisation of Cultural Heritage*, English 2.1, edited by Hugh Denard, 7 February 2009. The PDF cover says “Draft 2.1”; the Charter’s publication history identifies that version as official. Read printed pp. 8–9, §§4.4–4.7, and pp. 12–13, the glossary entries for *Intellectual transparency* and *Paradata*. The corresponding official web entries were also checked. [English edition](https://www.london-charter.org/media/files/london_charter_2_1_en.pdf) · [Version history](https://www.london-charter.org/history.html) · [Documentation](https://www.london-charter.org/documentation.html) · [Glossary](https://www.london-charter.org/glossary.html)

Section 4.4 asks what state or hypothesis a visualisation represents and where it is uncertain. Section 4.5 asks for the provenance of its sources. Section 4.6 concerns the decisions connecting material to interpretation. Section 4.7 makes the choice and rejection of methods part of what a reader can examine. The glossary connects intellectual transparency to understanding the reach of a claim. Paradata concerns the reasoning and interpretation that produced it.

The gallery is an authored scene. We borrow these documentation questions from heritage practice to examine its 128 available source views and the subset actually queried. The experimenters explain why they kept the target prediction fixed while changing the subset. Hashes identify the files on their manifest, and the query record identifies the files the checker used. Together these records allow a reader to examine the comparison. Reasons for placing cameras at these viewpoints, and omissions from the original design, can remain open. ## The Seville Principles on virtual archaeology

*The Seville Principles: International Principles of Virtual Archaeology*, English section of the trilingual text hosted by ICOMOS Spain. Its cover identifies the 19th ICOMOS General Assembly, New Delhi, December 2017. Resolution 19GA 2017/22 records endorsement. Read pp. 2–3 for remit and definitions, p. 6 for Principle 4, §§4.1–4.3, and p. 8 for Principle 7, §§7.1–7.4. [English text in the trilingual edition](https://icomos.es/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Seville-Principles-IN-ES-FR.pdf) · [Assembly resolution, p. 13](https://prod-admin.icomos.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/GA2017_Resolutions_EN_20180206finalcirc.pdf)

Section 4.1 calls for alternative virtual interpretations where hypotheses have equal scientific validity and endorses the leading hypothesis where they differ. Section 4.2 calls for making degrees of accuracy explicit. Section 4.3 distinguishes remains in place, reassembled material, physically rebuilt areas and virtual reconstruction. Principle 7 asks researchers to document sources, methods and reasoning so that other specialists can test the result.

For the gallery, the separate evaluation area lets another reader check the image against the held-out authored render. A disagreement can be traced to a particular camera and pixel rule. No archaeological remains, competing dating evidence or historical specialists entered this case. Accurate RGB would be equally possible for a carefully modelled fiction. The principles offer questions about disclosure and interpretation. Establishing historical authenticity would need material and historical evidence appropriate to the proposed claim.
